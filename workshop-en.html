ESP32-CAM Robot — workshop for under-14s

At a glance
Audience8–13, pitched at 10–13 with notes for younger
Length2½ hours, or four shorter sessions
GroupTwo children per robot. Three works; four does not
Per tableRobot, tablet or laptop, phone torch, two bright cards, 2 m of dark tape
Firmwarev2.0 or later
RobotESP32-CAM Robot

The robot already has a teaching shape: drive first and read your own program second, measure rather than be told, and see what the machine sees. This plan follows that arc rather than cutting across it.

Before the day

Networking is the biggest risk, not the robots. Eight robots each making their own ESP32-Robot-XXXX access point means eight tablets that must each join the right one — and children will connect to a neighbour's robot and conclude theirs is broken. Two ways out, best first:

  1. Put every robot on one classroom router, done once by you in Settings → Wi-Fi. Each then has its own address and nothing competes.
  2. If you must use the fallback access points, print the last four characters of each SSID on a large sticker on the chassis, matching the name in Settings → Robot. A child should be able to check “is this mine?” by looking.

Per robot, before they arrive:

  • Flash v2.0 and confirm the header says so.
  • Give it a name — an animal or a colour, never a hex suffix — and put that name on the chassis.
  • Set the speed limit to about 120. It is enforced in the firmware, so it holds however the command arrives, and it is the difference between a robot that can be caught and one that goes under a cupboard.
  • Lift the wheels and press forward. If either turns the wrong way, tick it under Wheel direction now. A robot that spins instead of driving will eat twenty minutes of a child's session.
  • Check the video is not black. If it is, reseat the camera ribbon.

The room. Work on the floor, not on tables — robots drive off tables. If it must be tables, tape a border 20 cm from the edge and make “stay inside the tape” the first rule of the day.

The blocks, one by one

0:00 — Meet your robot

Hand each pair a robot that already has a name, and have them find that name in the page header. That is the check that they are connected to theirs.

Then power-cycle it and watch the light together: three flashes, fast blips, then long-short-long. Tell them what each one means.

This pays off all afternoon. For the rest of the session, “look at the light” replaces “it is broken”.

0:10 — Drive

No instruction beyond the arrows and STOP. Let them drive badly for a while. Two rules, said once: stay inside the tape, and if it does something you do not like, let go — releasing an arrow stops the robot, and STOP is only the backup.

After ten minutes, the two-child cockpit: one holds the tablet and may only look at the video, the other watches the robot and may not touch the screen. They have to talk. This is where most children first understand that the robot's view is not their view.

0:30 — Why does it curve?

Ask them to drive straight to a mark. It will pull to one side. Do not explain — ask them why.

Then Settings → Robot → Measure them. The robot powers one wheel at a time, stepping up until they say it is moving, and two numbers come back. They will differ by twenty or thirty.

That difference is the lesson: identical parts are not identical. Let it sit before you give them the fix — Motor trim, and the ±1 buttons to take the pull out by hand.

Then the lane: two metres of tape, twenty centimetres wide, robot must stay inside. Trim is the only tool allowed.

1:05 — Teach it to repeat

Record, drive a short path, Stop. The list of steps appears. Play, and it does it again.

Then the part that matters: change one number and play it again. A pause of 1.4 s becomes 0.8 s. Delete a line. Predict out loud before every Play — the prediction is where the learning is, not the result.

Challenge: record a square. It will not come back to where it started. Ask them why; they met the answer half an hour ago.

Programs live in three slots on the robot, so a program follows the robot to another table. Let them swap and run each other's.

1:35 — What does it see?

Do Follow the torch as a whole-room activity first: dim the lights, everyone gets a torch, all the robots chase. Point at the strip drawn across the video and say — that is everything it knows. Then cover the lens and let them discover the stop rather than being told.

Then split by interest:

  • Follow the line — lay tape, and turn the Speed down first. When it loses a corner, the strip shows why.
  • Follow the card — show it a card, then hide the card and wave a hand. It stops. It is not following you. It is following orange. This is the best conversation of the day.
  • How fast does it turn? — for the older ones. Left and right will not match, for the reason they met at 0:30.

2:10 — Challenge and show

Pick one: fastest clean lap of the taped course; a program that visits three landmarks; or the whodunnit — swap one robot's motor leads beforehand and let a team diagnose it with Wheel direction. End with every pair showing one thing for sixty seconds.

Younger and older

8–10. Keep the lane, drop the numbers: “make it go straight by nudging these buttons” is enough. Skip the turn-rate game. Give the torch game more time — it is the one they will talk about at home.

11–13. Give them the wake-up numbers as numbers, and the turn-rate table as an experiment: measure at several speeds, plot requested against actual, and explain the gap. Ask them what the line follower is really seeing, then let them fool it on purpose.

Four sessions instead of one

  1. Drive, name, calibrate.
  2. Programs, ending with the square that does not close.
  3. The games, and building a course.
  4. A project of their own, presented. This is where it stops being a workshop and becomes theirs.

Four things to hold yourself to

Tell them about the camera. There is a camera in the room pointed at children, and photos can be taken. They stay in the browser for the session and are never stored on the robot — say that out loud, to the children and to any accompanying adult, at the start. Decide in advance whether photographs of faces are allowed at all.

Never update firmware during a session. Ten minutes of a rising glow and “do not touch it” is not an activity. Flash everything the night before.

Do not explain the LEDC timer bug. It is the best story in the repository and it is not for this audience. Save it for the teachers.

Let the failures happen. The square that does not close, the line follower losing a corner, two wheels that will not match — those are not things going wrong, they are the curriculum. The instinct to fix them quickly is the one to resist.
