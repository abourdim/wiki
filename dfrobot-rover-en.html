This article covers the continuous-rotation servo rover built on the DFRobot micro:Driver board. It shares its app with the rest of the series: the whole layout lives in the firmware and is sent to the browser on connect, the principle inherited from bit-rxy.

Overview

A rover that drives on two continuous-rotation servos, looks around with an ultrasonic sensor on a third servo, and shows what it knows on a 128×32 screen. You drive it from a web page over Bluetooth — nothing to install, no account, no WiFi.

The robot owns the layout. The app is a plain renderer: it connects, asks the rover what controls it has, and draws whatever comes back. There is nothing to configure in the browser, and no special build of the app for this robot.

What sets this rover apart from the others in the series: 360° servos instead of DC motors, which makes trim unavoidable; a head that sweeps the room; and a screen that finally earns its place — self-test, face, or radar scope.

Quick start

  1. Switch the rover on: the self-test shows for two and a half seconds.
  2. Open the app in Chrome or Edge — Safari and Firefox have no Web Bluetooth.
  3. Press Connect and pick the micro:bit whose name is on the robot’s screen.
  4. Wait for the layout transfer (~10 s the first time, cached afterwards), then drive.

Step by step: clone, flash & test

1. Clone the repository

git clone https://github.com/abourdim/dfrobot-rover.git
cd dfrobot-rover

The firmware is a single file: firmware/rover-remote.ts. There is no local build — MakeCode compiles in the browser, so a clean paste is the build step.

2. Add the two extensions

Open makecode.microbit.org, start a project, then Extensions. Paste each URL in turn:

https://github.com/DFRobot/pxt-motor
https://github.com/tinkertanker/pxt-oled-ssd1306

Paste the URL, do not search by name. DFRobot published theirs as plain motor, the most generic name on the platform, so a search returns a pile of look-alikes — and the wrong one compiles cleanly and moves nothing.

Not pxt-neopixel. The light strip is wired to P15 but nothing drives it: that extension will not share this board with Bluetooth, and between a light strip and a robot you can steer, the robot wins.

3. Add the invisible dependency: settings

Wheel trim is stored in the micro:bit’s flash, which needs the settings library. Searching Extensions for it finds nothing: it ships with the editor but is marked hidden. Add it by hand:

  1. Open Explorer, bottom left of the editor
  2. Click pxt.json
  3. Add one line to dependencies:
"settings": "*"

It goes last, so the line above needs a comma added. Without it every settings. line fails with Cannot find name ‘settings’. Adding Data Logger does not help: that depends on flashlog, not on settings.

4. Bluetooth project settings

Gear icon → Project Settings. Get these wrong and it looks like a broken app rather than a wrong setting.

SettingValueWhy
No Pairing RequiredONOtherwise every child must pair their board, then reset it just to flash again.
Bluetooth UART serviceONThe only service used: the whole protocol is lines of text.
Every other BLE serviceOFFEach costs memory and advertising space for nothing.

Radio and Bluetooth cannot coexist on this hardware: adding Bluetooth removes the radio package, and MakeCode asks you to accept that.

5. Paste and flash

Switch to the JavaScript view, paste all of firmware/rover-remote.ts over what is there, and download. The LEDs scroll the version at boot — that is how you confirm what is actually flashed instead of assuming.

6. Read the self-test

Every power-up, before anything connects, for two and a half seconds:

Rover R1-v19
Driver 0x40  ok
Sonar  42 cm
Trim  L +7  R -7

A rover fails in three ways: sensor lead off, driver board not seen, flat pack. Driver 0x40 MISSING means the board or its connector; Sonar NO ECHO means the sensor lead, usually the 3V3 one. The test really does probe the I²C bus — it is not a guess.

The screen is deliberately not on the list: if you can read the list, the screen works.

7. Connect

Press Connect. The browser chooser lists names like BBC micro:bit [gazug] — exactly what the rover shows on its own screen when disconnected, so there is no guessing which one is yours in a room full of robots.

8. Switch panels

The Level selector is on every panel: you move between exercises with a dropdown instead of an adult reflashing.

PanelWhat’s on itTransfer
BeginnerPad, STOP, distance, alert. Nothing else.~2.8 s
Expert (default)Everything the rover can do — 31 widgets~10.3 s
DriveJog each wheel, watch which way it turns, trim it straight~4.8 s
DistanceGauge, alert, graph and the sweep head~3.5 s
ScreenType a message, choose the screen mode~1.9 s

9. Trim it straight — the step you cannot skip

Two 360° servos never run at matched speeds, so a new rover always curves. Each wheel has three ways at the same number, because getting a robot straight is really three different jobs:

ControlWhat it’s for
Trim L / Trim RGet close in one drag
L − 1 / L + 1, R − 1 / R + 1The one that matters: “still pulls slightly left” is a single degree, and one degree of servo pulse is the floor of the API
L = / R =Type back a number you wrote down

All three drive the same value and the firmware echoes it back to all three, so they cannot disagree. Expect single digits; more than about 10 means something mechanical.

The rover remembers it itself. Trim is written to its own flash: it survives a battery change, a reflash, and a move to a different tablet. It is saved two seconds after you stop adjusting, never on every movement of the slider — flash has a finite number of erase cycles and a slider drag is a stream of values.

10. Choose what the screen shows

The Screen selector offers four settings, kept in flash beside the trim.

SettingShows
StatusName, distance, trim, speed (default)
FaceTwo eyes
AutoThe text until the app connects, the eyes afterwards
RadarThe polar radar scope

Auto is the one worth choosing: before a connection the only useful thing on that screen is which micro:bit this is; afterwards the app is already showing every number.

For the radar, also set Head to Sweep: without it the head stays at 90° and only one bearing can ever be drawn.

Understanding the code (for beginners)

The hardware seam

Everything board-specific sits in three functions. Changing driver board means rewriting those three and nothing else:

wheels(l, r)   // drive mix -> two servo pulses
wheelsStop()   // both wheels stopped, trim included
pingCm()       // one ultrasonic reading, or the “no echo” sentinel

Everything below that seam is the shared rxy stack: Bluetooth, the chunked config transfer, the layout cache, telemetry, the drive watchdog.

The mirror that makes it all work

let pl = WHEEL_STOP + Math.idiv(l * 90, DRIVE_SPEED_MAX) + trimL
let pr = WHEEL_STOP - Math.idiv(r * 90, DRIVE_SPEED_MAX) - trimR

The right servo is mounted facing the other way, so “forward” is down from 90 on that side and up on the left. Trim is therefore added on the left and subtracted on the right, so a positive number means “more forward” on both wheels. Adding it to both is exactly what made the series’ ESP32 rover curve.

How the layout reaches the browser

The layout is a Base64 string compiled into the firmware, one per panel. On connect the app asks for a revision fingerprint first. If the browser already has that revision cached it answers CFGOK and nothing more is transferred; otherwise come CFGBEGIN, the chunks, then CFGEND.

The transfer is driven from the main loop, never from the receive callback: streaming a layout from inside the BLE callback is what historically broke reconnects.

The screen: two ways to draw

Text goes through the OLED extension. Simple, but slow: ten I²C transactions per character, and the extension exposes no cursor move — clear() is the only way back to the top left, so every repaint is the whole screen. Hence the rule: lines are repainted only when they actually differ, and distance is rounded to 5 cm so sensor jitter alone cannot trigger one.

The face and the radar cannot be drawn that way. drawFilledCircle() calls drawLine() per column, and every drawShape() spends six commands plus a write per column-page: one filled circle is several hundred transactions. So they keep their own 512-byte buffer and push whole frames — eight writes of 65 bytes for the entire screen.

Both freeze while the wheels turn: a frame is 512 bytes on the same bus as the driver board, inside the loop that feeds the drive watchdog. That is also why the dizziness lands after a spin rather than during it.

The face

ExpressionTrigger
Pupils follow the headAlways — they track Look
BlinkEvery 2.5–6 s
Worried, brows upSomething closer than 25 cm

Asleep20 s with no driving
Alarmed, pupils shrunkPicked up or tipped past 45°

The worried brow is a wedge cut out of the top of the eye, deepest at the outer edge so the inner ends ride up. Cut the other way, the same shape reads as angry.

The radar scope

A ring at 30 cm, another at 100 cm, a beam on the live head angle, and detections that persist for five seconds: a sweep builds a picture of the room instead of showing one reading.

The disc is stretched sideways by two. A half-disc that fits 32 rows can only have a 31-pixel radius and would leave two-thirds of the glass black. The distance scale is not linear — the first ten centimetres take a quarter of the radius — and it is the same scale the app uses, so both displays agree about what “close” looks like.

A no-echo reading is never plotted: that would draw a wall at maximum range right around an empty room.

Free pins: P2 P12 P13 P14 P16. P8 carries the tone output, moved off P0 because P0 is the sonar trigger: a beep would fire spurious pings.

Protocol

Lines of text over the UART service. Three families cover everything:

MessageDirectionMeaning
SET <id> <value>App → robotA control moved
UPD <id> <value>Robot → appA value changed on the robot
GETCFG · CFGBEGIN · CFG · CFGENDBothLayout transfer

The D-pad does not use it: each state is encoded as one ASCII byte, making a complete motor command two bytes. Drive latency is the one place where this readable protocol would have cost too much.
