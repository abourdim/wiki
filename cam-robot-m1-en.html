Like m2, this robot serves its own page. But here you do not even type the address: join its Wi-Fi and the page opens by itself.

m1 is not an earlier m2 — it is a different robot. Same ESP32-CAM module, an entirely different motor board, and therefore opposite capabilities. m1 cannot reverse; in exchange it follows a colour, makes an engine noise, and opens its own page — three things m2 does not do.

The division is clean: m1 is the better software, m2 is the better electronics. This article covers what belongs to m1 and points at m2 for the machinery they share.

Overview

Like m2, m1 is its own server: the firmware carries the whole page, serves it on port 80, streams video on port 81, and nothing is installed. The compressed interface is 41 KB here.

What really separates the two robots fits in one sentence: m1 has the better app and the worse motor board. It follows a colour, it sings, it opens its own page — but it cannot reverse, because its electronics will not allow it.

That is a useful comparison in a workshop precisely because it does not run the way people expect. A higher version number does not mean "better everywhere": m2 gained an H-bridge and reverse, and did not carry over the colour following or the sound.

Quick start

  1. Power the robot. It tries the configured network for 10 seconds, then opens its access point.
  2. Join ESP32-Robot-XXXX, password 88888888.
  3. Type nothing. The phone checks whether it has internet, the robot answers, and a sign-in page opens by itself. Press Open the robot. See The captive portal.
  4. Press an arrow and hold it.
  5. Open Follow, tap a red ball in the picture, then press Follow: the robot goes to it on its own.

The board and its pinout: one MOSFET per motor

This is the heart of what makes m1 different. The board carries one low-side MOSFET per motor. One MOSFET switches one motor, in one direction only.

SignalPinRole
Left motor gateGPIO13PWM, one direction only
Right motor gateGPIO12PWM, one direction only
Flash LEDGPIO4Lighting and the boot blink

The consequence is direct, and it is not a software matter:

  • Forward: yes.
  • Steering: yes, by stopping one wheel.
  • Stop: yes.
  • Reverse: no. No firmware can reverse a motor through a single low-side transistor.
Reverse is disabled deliberately in the interface, and it is not a limitation to work around: it is the circuit. True bidirectional control needs an H-bridge — a DRV8833, or the TB6612FNG that m2 carries.

One more difference in how it drives: m1 gives a short full-power kick at startup to overcome stiction, where m2 uses a sixteen-step ramp. Both solve the same problem — a loaded motor does not start at low duty — from opposite ends, and the full-power kick is much harder on the supply.

Why m1 escaped the LEDC timer bug

m2 reset for a long time whenever a motor started, because its fixed PWM channels 8 and 9 landed on the timer esp_camera_init() reprograms to 20 MHz at one bit of resolution. The question arises naturally for m1, which runs its motors on the same chip, beside the same camera.

m1 never had the problem, and that is luck.

Where m2 reserves fixed channels, m1 simply calls analogWrite() on GPIO13 and GPIO12 and lets the Arduino core choose. And the analogWrite() allocator counts downward from the top:

static int cnt_channel = LEDC_CHANNELS;   // 16
...
cnt_channel--;
channel = cnt_channel;                    // 15, then 14, then 13...

So the first motor gets channel 15 and the second gets channel 14. Under the rule timer = (channel / 2) % 4, both land on timer 3 of the low-speed group. The camera holds timer 0. Nothing overlaps.

The lesson is almost annoying: by explicitly reserving channels 8 to 11 in order to be rigorous, m2 collided with the camera, while m1 got away with deciding nothing at all. Explicit allocation is still the right call — but then you have to know what you are allocating.

m1's safety rests on there being only two analogWrite() pins. Every extra pin walks the counter down one step: by the seventh, the allocator reaches channel 9 and m2's left motor comes back to haunt m1. This is not headroom to spend without thinking.

Following a colour

The third view in the header, beside Drive and Program. The robot drives itself towards something a child chose by tapping it on the video.

  1. Open Follow.
  2. Tap the video on the thing to follow — a red ball, a blue cup. The swatch shows the colour that was picked up.
  3. Press Follow.

The robot turns towards the colour, drives at it, and stops when it fills enough of the picture. A blue box on the video shows what it found.

It is not clever, and that is the lesson

Eight times a second the page draws the video into a 64×48 canvas, walks all 3072 pixels, and keeps the ones whose colour matches. Then it steers towards their middle:

  • middle third of the frame — forward;
  • left of that — turn left;
  • right of that — turn right;
  • enough matching pixels to fill Close enough — stop;
  • nothing matching for a second — stop, and the status reads lost it.

How fussy is how far a colour may be from the one picked and still count. Wide enough and a red ball and an orange chair are the same thing; narrow enough and a cloud passing the window loses the ball. That slider is most of the activity: it makes visible that a machine does not "see" an object, it compares numbers.

All of that computation happens in the browser, not on the ESP32. The robot receives only steering commands — exactly the ones a child would send.

Robot sound

The Sound pill in the header. The robot has no speaker: this is the tablet making the noise, which is why it can be an engine rather than a beeper and costs the ESP32 nothing.

  • An engine hum whose pitch follows the PWM the motors are being given.
  • A short chirp on stop.
  • A descending buzz when the motion timeout fires, alongside the red TIMEOUT on the video.

The pitch is the reason it exists. Run one motor up slowly and the note climbs; the stretch where the note is clearly rising and the wheel still is not turning is exactly the stiction that Find the wake-up number puts a number on. A replayed program sounds like the drive that recorded it.

Pressing a direction answers immediately from the slider values, and the robot's own report a poll later corrects it — so a lost command is audible the same way it is visible.

Off by default, and remembered per browser. Ten robots humming in one room is a teacher's problem, and switching it on deliberately is part of the lesson. Browsers refuse to let a page make noise until somebody has touched it, so the first press is what starts it.

The captive portal

Nobody has to type 192.168.4.1.

While the fallback AP is up, the robot answers every DNS lookup with its own address. A phone that has just joined checks whether it can reach the internet, is answered by the robot instead, decides the network wants a sign-in, and opens a page by itself.

That page is deliberately tiny — the robot name and an Open the robot link — rather than the full interface. The sign-in browser an operating system opens is a restricted one (CustomTabs on Android), where the video stream and Web Serial both behave badly, so the link is there to get out of it into the real browser. It also means the 70 KB UI is never dragged through a connectivity check.

  • Only on the fallback AP. On the configured network the robot is a guest and has no business answering DNS for anything: a genuine 404 there stays a 404.
  • The robot provides no route to the internet, so a joined device will keep saying it has no connection. That is correct, and it is what makes the sign-in page appear.
  • If the portal does not open on some device, nothing is lost: open 192.168.4.1 by hand.

Driving: hold, and the dead man's switch

Identical to m2, and for the same reasons: a held button repeats its command every 250 ms, the robot stops itself if it hears nothing for 600 ms, the release sends stop twice 150 ms apart, and losing focus or hiding the tab stops as well.

Colour following and program replay go through those same functions. A robot driving itself therefore obeys the same guard as a child pressing a button — which is what makes the autonomy safe here, rather than a safety layer bolted on top.

What m1 shares with m2

Both robots share the same frame, described in detail in the m2 article:

  • Record and replay — drive by hand and the drive becomes an editable list of instructions, kept on the robot.
  • Finding the wake-up number — the guided activity that steps up by 5 until the wheel turns, putting a number on the fact that two identical motors are not identical.
  • Photos, robot name, speed limit, camera settings in NVS.
  • The two sidebars, Settings and Debug, with the 64-event history.
  • UART0 console at 115200 8N1, and browser OTA with password 88888888 on min_spiffs.csv partitions.

Clone, flash & test

git clone https://github.com/abourdim/wdi_esp32_cam_robot_m1
cd wdi_esp32_cam_robot_m1
pio run -e esp32cam
pio run -e esp32cam -t upload --upload-port COM31

Same constraints as m2: a 3.3 V USB-TTL adapter, GPIO0 held low while powering up, and upload speed at 115200. The web interface is a literal inside the sketch, and tools/gzip_ui.py regenerates index_html_gz.h after any change.

Troubleshooting

The robot will not reverse

Not a fault. There is one MOSFET per motor: see the pinout. To reverse, you need m2 or another H-bridge.

Colour following wanders off

Widen or tighten How fussy. Too wide and it latches onto anything vaguely the right hue; too narrow and it loses the target the moment the light changes. Remember the comparison runs on a 64×48 image: a distant object is only a few pixels there.

The captive portal does not open

Some devices do not launch the sign-in page. Open 192.168.4.1 by hand; everything works the same. Check too that you are on the fallback AP and not the configured network, where the portal is deliberately inactive.

The sound will not start

Browsers forbid a page from making noise before an interaction. Press a button once, then switch Sound back on.

The board resets when a motor starts

On m1 the suspect is not the LEDC timer (see above) but the full-power startup kick, which hits the 5 V rail far harder than a ramp does. Check the supply and the decoupling before looking for a software fault.
